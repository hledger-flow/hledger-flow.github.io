Part 1: Try the example

This is the first part of a six-part tutorial.

The Story of an African Chef

Section titled “The Story of an African Chef”

Meet Gawie de Groot. Gawie is a successful mopane worm chef, based in the northernmost section of the Kruger National Park.

In times gone by he used to work in a big city as an IT professional. But after a few years he decided to choose a quieter life in the bushveld.

He now works at a renowned bush restaurant aptly named the “Grillerige Groen Goggatjie”.

Demand for his traditional African cuisine has sky-rocketed, with visitors from all over Zimbabwe, Mozambique and South Africa coming to enjoy his dishes.

Business has been good the last few years, but Gawie wants to make sure that he’ll also be OK when business is down.

It is time for Gawie to take charge of his finances.

Start a ledger

Section titled “Start a ledger”

Gawie installed hledger-flow and runs it to see what it says:

He makes a folder for his finances. His IT years taught him to keep anything he cares about in Git, so he starts a repository first. Then every change hledger-flow makes shows up as a diff he can read before he commits it.

init made a small but complete ledger:

  • import/gawie/mybank/checking is an example account. Its folders are the owner, the bank and the account. 1-in/ holds a sample statement, filed under the current year, and mybank-checking.rules tells hledger how to read it. hledger-flow takes the owner name from your login.
  • The _manual_ folders, at every level, hold starter journals for transactions you type in yourself. <year>-opening.journal and <year>-closing.journal hold an account’s opening and closing balances.
  • directives.journal declares common currencies and account types for the whole ledger.
  • prices/ holds starter files for exchange rates.

The starters are only comments with an example in each, so they change nothing until Gawie fills them in. He commits the new ledger:

Import the example

Section titled “Import the example”

init ended by naming the next step:

The CSV rules can read the sample statement as it is, so the import goes straight from 1-in/ to 3-journal/. Part 5 shows the optional step in between. A preprocess script handles cleanup that CSV rules can’t do, and the cleaned CSV goes in 2-preprocessed/.

The sample statement became an hledger journal in 3-journal/. The *-include.journal and all-years.journal files tie every journal together, so the top-level all-years.journal holds the whole ledger. hledger-flow writes them on every import; don’t edit them.

Report on the example

Section titled “Report on the example”

report saves a set of hledger reports for each owner and year, plus combined ones for all owners under reports/all/. The example’s purchase and refund cancel out, so its balance sheet and income statement show zeros. Gawie’s own statements will fill them in.

Ask status what’s next

Section titled “Ask status what’s next”

status lists each account with its statements, says what to do next, and ends with the versions and settings it found. It only reads, so git status printed nothing. Gawie can run it at any point. He’ll deal with the note about LEDGER_FILE in part 2.

Make the account your own

Section titled “Make the account your own”

Gawie starts with the easiest of his accounts: his Kudu Bank credit card. Kudu’s online banking exports a tidy CSV, with a header row, one row per transaction and dates hledger already understands:

He follows status’s advice. He keeps his owner folder, renames the bank and account folders, and removes the sample statement and its rules:

init made the starter journals and prices for the current year, but Gawie’s statements are from 2016. He removes the starters and the example’s reports. He’ll add the files he needs for 2016 later in the tutorial. If your own statements are from this year, keep the starters.

Then he files his first statement in the folder for its year, import/<owner>/<bank>/<account>/1-in/<year>/, and asks status again:

Git sees the renamed folders as renames (R) and the example’s files as deletions (D). The example journal in 3-journal/ moved with its folder; the next import deals with it.

Status’s next step is a rules file for the new statement. That’s where part 2 begins.
